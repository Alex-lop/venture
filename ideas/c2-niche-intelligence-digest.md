# Niche Intelligence Digest

**Slug:** c2-niche-intelligence-digest  |  **Track:** C  |  **Researched:** 2026-08-30  |  **Status:** researched

## One-line pitch
A weekly AI-assisted email digest of Massachusetts municipal built-environment activity (BPDA Article 80 filings, permits, zoning board decisions, RFPs) sold to Boston-area developers, contractors, brokers and land-use attorneys — free to read, monetized by local sponsors, and functioning as the distribution engine and lead magnet for B1 (municipal signals product).

## Specific buyer
Two distinct buyers, and they are **not** equally reachable.

**Buyer A — the subscriber (does not pay, at least not first).**
Title: Business Development Director, Preconstruction Manager, Principal/Partner at a 10–200 person Greater Boston development, GC, architecture, or land-use law firm; also acquisitions associates at regional CRE shops. They read Banker & Tradesman, Boston Real Estate Times, The Real Reporter, BLDUP, and CommonWealth Beacon. They hang out:
- **Online:** archBOSTON forum (archboston.com — "Threads: 5,646 / Messages: 490,672 / Members: 2,943", fetched 2026-08-30); LinkedIn (the dominant channel for this trade — Boston Real Estate Times and The Real Reporter both distribute there); BLDUP project pages; Universal Hub comment threads on Boston development stories.
- **Offline (Boston, walkable/T-accessible for the principal):** NAIOP Massachusetts events; Boston Society for Architecture (BSA Space, 290 Congress St); ULI Boston/New England programs; Greater Boston Real Estate Board; BPDA board meetings and Article 80 public meetings (published on boston.gov/public-notices and boston.legistar.com — open to the public, free, and physically attendable); Neighborhood Association of the Back Bay (NABB) posts BPDA Article 80 project public meetings publicly; A Better City events.

**Buyer B — the sponsor (this is who actually pays).**
Title: Marketing Director or Owner at a Boston-area vendor selling *into* buyer A: permit expediters, zoning/land-use law firms, construction lenders, title companies, surveyors, environmental consultants, owner's-rep firms, construction-tech vendors. They already buy this exact audience: Boston Real Estate Times sells them $1,200–$1,695/month display slots.

## Reachability (50 qualified buyers in 30 days, $0)

Reaching 50 *subscribers* is easy. Reaching 50 *qualified buyers* (i.e. sponsors) is the real test, and it is the reason to run this at all.

**Subscriber side (free, no spam, no DM blasts):**
- **archBOSTON forum** — 2,943 members, 490,672 messages, threads on named Boston projects updated daily (fetched 2026-08-30). Participate genuinely in project threads; link the digest in a profile/signature, not as drive-by promotion.
- **LinkedIn** — the actual home of this trade. Post the digest's top 3 items weekly as a native post. Boston Real Estate Times and The Real Reporter both run their distribution here.
- **BPDA public meetings and Article 80 project meetings** — published free at boston.gov/public-notices, boston.legistar.com, and listed by NABB (nabbonline.org/bpda-article-80-project-public-meetings/). The principal lives in Boston and can physically attend; these rooms contain developers, their attorneys, and their consultants, and attendance is free.
- **Universal Hub** — high-traffic Boston civic comment community, fetchable and active.
- **BSA Space (290 Congress St), NAIOP MA, ULI Boston/NE events** — student rates or free public programs; the single strongest asymmetry the principal has is that a Northeastern student can walk into Boston industry events that a remote competitor cannot.

**Sponsor side (the 50 that matter):**
Boston Real Estate Times' and Banker & Tradesman's current advertisers are a **public, enumerable list** — every banner on those sites names a vendor already paying $1,200–1,695/mo to reach this exact audience. That is a pre-qualified prospect list of well over 50, obtainable in an afternoon at $0, and reachable by a Boston student in person at the same events their buyers attend.

**Honest caveat:** none of this is a *scaled* channel. There is no product-led growth loop, no open-source funnel, no viral surface. Growth is linear in the principal's hours — which are the scarce resource.

## Wedge
**Pre-sell one sponsorship slot for a four-issue run, before writing issue one.**

"The Boston Filing" — a Friday email listing every Article 80 filing, BPDA board approval, and ZBA decision in Boston that week, with the developer, the address, the unit/SF count, and the project team where public. One sponsor slot per issue.

Pitch to a Boston permit expediter, land-use boutique, or construction lender: **$200 for four issues** (vs. the $1,200/mo they pay Boston Real Estate Times), positioned as a pilot in front of a small, high-intent, hand-built local list. This is the smallest thing one buyer would pay for this month, it costs $0 to attempt, and it is a real falsification test: **if nobody will pay $200, the entire track is dead in two weeks for zero dollars.**

## Build estimate
**1.5–3 agent-days to a sellable MVP.** This is the cheapest idea in the portfolio to build and the most expensive to distribute.

Components:
- Public-source ingestion: BPDA/Boston Legistar agendas + minutes (boston.legistar.com), boston.gov public notices, Boston Open Data permit dataset, plus 3–5 surrounding municipalities' Accela/CivicPlus portals. Respect robots.txt; all sources are published public records.
- LLM summarization pass producing one-line-per-item entries with a source link on every line.
- Human (principal) review pass before every send — mandatory, both for accuracy and because the editorial judgment *is* the product.
- Substack (or Buttondown) for send, archive, and list ownership. No custom frontend.
- A one-page sponsor kit: audience description, open rate, rate card, and a screenshot of a sample issue.

`Reusable assets: Same as B1 (durable queue, snapshot-diff, citation validator) once the digest is generated rather than hand-compiled.`

## Unit economics

**Sponsor model (the real one):**
- Price: $150–250 per issue at <1,000 subscribers, per the beehiiv benchmark (<5K subs → $50–250/placement).
- Weekly cadence → 4.3 issues/mo → **$650–1,075/mo gross at one slot per issue, sold out.** Realistically 40–60% sell-through in year one → **$260–650/mo.**
- Costs: Substack $0/mo (no cut on sponsorship revenue, only on subscriptions). LLM API for weekly summarization of ~60–100 municipal documents: assuming ~150k input tokens and ~15k output tokens per issue on a mid-tier model, ~$0.60–1.50/issue → **~$3–7/mo**. Hosting $0 (Substack). Domain ~$1.25/mo amortized.
- **Total burn: under $10/mo. Gross margin: ~98%.** Comfortably inside the $40/mo cap.
- The real cost is unpriced: **~4 hrs/week** (2 to produce and review, 2 to sell), i.e. **one third of the principal's 12 weekly hours** for $300–600/mo.

**Paid-subscription model (do not build this in year one):**
- $20/mo × 25 subscribers = $500 MRR, minus Substack 10% + Stripe ≈ $430 net.
- Requires ~4,030 free subscribers at the 0.62% median. Not reachable at 12 hrs/week.

**As a lead magnet for B1 (the actual justification):**
- If the digest converts even **3 readers** into $99/mo B1 subscriptions, that is **$297 MRR** — matching the entire sponsorship business at a fraction of the selling effort, because you are selling a tool at a price the $5,936/yr manual cost actually supports.

## Risks

- **Legal — low.** Content is derived from published municipal records (BPDA filings, permits, board decisions) which are public by statute. Risk is manageable but must be respected: per the ethics constraint, issues surface **business-relevant facts about projects and firms**, never profiles of the private individuals named in permits and abutter filings. Robots.txt and ToS honored on every source; nothing behind a login or paywall.
- **Accuracy liability — medium, and the sharpest risk here.** An LLM misreading a filed unit count or misattributing a developer, in a publication that business decisions are made on, is a credibility-ending error. Evidence it's easy to get wrong: one commenter (HN 47260508, 2026-03-05) on the same data — "The estimated cost field is a work of creative fiction. A $200 million data center will s[how]...". Mitigation is non-negotiable: a source link on every line, a "verify at the source before acting" footer, and a human review pass before every send.
- **Platform dependency — low.** Substack and Buttondown both allow full CSV export of the subscriber list; the list is a genuinely portable owned asset. The 10% Substack cut only applies to subscription revenue, and this plan doesn't have any.
- **Incumbent response — low probability, high impact if it happens.** Banker & Tradesman (The Warren Group), Boston Real Estate Times (13,700 subs) and BLDUP already own this beat with staff and, in B&T's case, proprietary registry data. They are unlikely to notice a 500-subscriber newsletter. But they will not be displaced, and the same advertiser pool is finite — a sponsor spending $1,695/mo with BRET has limited appetite for a second Boston line item.
- **The real risk is quiet failure, not blowup.** The counter-evidence is strong and consistent: "the last thing they need is another newsletter or report" (HN 44704916); "aggregation beats adding another newsletter to the pile" (HN 44704423); the median newsletter converts 0.62% of readers to paid. This idea most likely consumes 4 hrs/week for 12 months and produces a few hundred dollars a month and a small list.

## Kill criteria

1. **Sponsor test (the cheap one, run first): if no Boston-area vendor pays $200 for a four-issue sponsorship by 2026-10-15, kill the standalone-revenue thesis.** Requires zero build — a one-page rate card and 20 in-person/email conversations with vendors already advertising in Boston Real Estate Times.
2. **If fewer than 250 free subscribers by 2026-11-30**, the distribution thesis is dead and the digest cannot function as a lead magnet for B1 either. 250 is deliberately low: it is roughly 1.8% of Boston Real Estate Times' list, and it is the floor at which a $150 sponsor slot is defensible.
3. **If fewer than 2 B1/B2 qualified leads are attributable to the digest by 2026-12-31**, the lead-magnet justification fails too and the 4 hrs/week should be redeployed entirely into B1.

Any single miss demotes this to a marketing channel run at under 1 hr/week. All three missed = shut it down.

## Verdict

**This is not a business; it is a marketing channel with a revenue rounding error attached — and it should be run as exactly that, not scored as a venture.** The evidence is unusually clean on this point. The paid-tier path is arithmetically closed: beehiiv's own 2026 data puts the median free-to-paid conversion at 0.62%, meaning $500 MRR at $20/mo needs roughly 4,030 free subscribers, which is not reachable at 12 hrs/week in a year — and every vertical digest that actually scaled (Construction Dive at 483,500 readers, Banking Dive at 250,500) gave up on subscriptions and went free-plus-ads. The sponsor path is real but small: the industry benchmark for a sub-5,000-subscriber newsletter is $50–250 per placement, so a fully sold-out weekly digest grosses $650–1,075/mo at best and more likely $300–600, in exchange for about four hours a week — a third of the principal's total capacity — much of it spent on sales, which is the one activity the agent cannot do for him. Against that, the two genuinely valuable findings are that Boston advertisers are *already* paying $1,200–1,695/month to reach 13,700 Boston CRE readers via Boston Real Estate Times, which hands over a pre-qualified, enumerable sponsor list at zero cost; and that the manual cost being displaced — roughly $5,936/yr of a Boston compliance analyst's time at $38.05/hr — comfortably justifies a $99/mo *product* while justifying almost nothing at newsletter prices. Both of those findings point the same direction, and it is not at C2. The right move is the cheap falsification test first: build nothing, print a rate card, and try to sell one four-issue sponsorship for $200 to a Boston permit expediter by mid-October. If that sells, run the digest at under an hour a week as B1's distribution engine and never build a paid tier. If it doesn't, the whole track dies for zero dollars and roughly two weeks — which is the best property this idea has. One correction to the plan itself: it assumes beehiiv fits the $40/month budget. It does not. beehiiv's free Launch tier locks paid subscriptions, the ad network, and automations behind Scale at $43–49/month; the correct stack under this constraint is Substack at $0 fixed, or Buttondown at roughly $18/month.
